Problems I solve

Two requests hit the same balance at the same moment, and money goes out twice.

Double-spend is a design problem, not a bug to patch. I build exactly-once, idempotent processing: each request creates an attempt record keyed to a unique identifier, and the ledger deduction is guarded by a rowversion optimistic-concurrency check — so the update only lands if nothing moved underneath it. Nearly right stops being an available outcome.

The whole business runs on a legacy platform nobody wants to touch.

Rewrites are where companies go to die. I replaced a legacy banking platform with a modern white-label engine while it kept trading — moving the processing flow across piece by piece, never taking the business dark.

One codebase has to serve multiple legal entities, and the regulator wants them separated.

Multi-tenancy done properly is invisible until it's audited. Configuration-driven, one codebase: a database per legal entity, a schema per brand, resolved per request behind load-balanced instances — purpose-built for SOX-aligned segregation rather than retrofitted to it.

The ledger has to stay fast, and you have to keep seven years of it.

Those two requirements fight each other. I use tiered partitioning with progressive cold-archiving — detaching yearly partitions onto colder servers — plus in-memory tables and tuned fill factors on the high-contention paths. Regulatory retention met; the hot tables never feel it.

You need to accept money through 150 different providers, and none of them behave the same.

I work on the layer that makes that plurality somebody else's problem — abstracting 150+ money processors behind one contract, routing online callback and polling flows alongside offline ones like USSD reconciliation, without leaking provider quirks into the domain.

Nobody can run the system locally, so testing happens against live data.

That isn't inconvenience, it's exposure. On an acquired platform locked to its vendor's cloud, I introduced .NET Aspire and a synthetic data layer — which broke the hosting dependency, made local development possible, and eliminated testing against live production data before the migration onto the company's own tenancy.

Engineering and the business agree on a plan, then discover they meant different things.

I've written business, user, functional and technical specifications since my first year in the industry — each pitched to the audience that has to sign it off, with the diagrams that make disagreement visible before it becomes rework. Translating between stakeholders and a domain model is the part I'm most often asked back for.

How I build

P·01

Correctness before cleverness

Under concurrency, the clever version is usually the one that loses money. Money is decimal, always.

P·02

The schema is the design

The query plan is the proof. Most performance problems are already visible if someone reads them.

P·03

Decisions get written down

Anything hard to reverse becomes an ADR — the context, the options including doing nothing, and what it now costs us.

P·04

Ship through the change

Trunk-based with feature flags, released daily behind canaries. Migrations that never take the business offline.

P·05

Specify for the reader

The same decision written three ways for three audiences. Ambiguity found in a document is far cheaper than ambiguity found in production.

P·06

Argue, then commit

I work best with decision ownership and a short path from problem to production. I'll push back on process that doesn't move the outcome — and commit fully once it's settled.
